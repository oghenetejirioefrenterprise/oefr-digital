**Exact title:**
`Got a Board of Nursing complaint? The first 72 hours, the deadline people miss, and how to respond without making it worse`

**Body:**

> Getting a "notice of investigation" letter from your state Board is terrifying, and the entire internet basically yells "hire a lawyer." For serious stuff — patient harm, anything involving drugs/diversion, criminal, or boundary/abuse allegations — that advice is 100% correct, stop reading and call a license-defense attorney today. But a lot of complaints are minor and procedural, and people freeze and either miss the deadline or write something that hurts them. Here's the part that actually helps, free:
>
> **1. Find the deadline and put it on your calendar the day the letter arrives.** Most boards give you a fixed, short window (commonly 10–30 days) to submit a written response to the allegations. Missing it can let the board proceed without your side. This is the single most common avoidable mistake.
>
> **2. Do NOT call the investigator to "explain" off the cuff.** You have the right to respond *in writing*, on your timeline. An unprepared phone call where you ramble to "clear it up" is how a minor issue becomes an admission. Respond in writing, after you've organized.
>
> **3. Separate facts from admissions before you write.** Write down what objectively happened — dates, what the chart says, who was present, your policies/CE records — separately from any conclusion about fault. Your response should be factual, specific, calm, and respectful, and it should answer each allegation. It should not editorialize or guess.
>
> **4. Know your due-process rights.** An investigation is not a finding. You generally have the right to notice of the allegations, the right to respond, the right to be represented by counsel, and the right to a hearing if formal charges are filed. Knowing that lowers the panic.
>
> **5. Know the bright line for "get a lawyer now."** Any allegation involving patient harm, medication diversion, impairment, criminal conduct, fraud, or abuse/boundary violations → do not DIY; the downside (license loss, NPDB report that follows you across states) is too high.
>
> Wishing a calm outcome to anyone going through this — it's more survivable than that letter makes it feel.
